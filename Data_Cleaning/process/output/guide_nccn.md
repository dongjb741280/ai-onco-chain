• Footnote kkk revised: In the setting of oligometastatic breast cancer, the currently available data do not support ablative metastasis-directed RT (ie, stereotactic body radiation therapy [SBRT]) for
extending overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS). In some cases, SBRT may be preferred over palliative RT to provide more durable local control and pain relief. Optimal management of
oligometastatic stage IV breast cancers remains uncertain. Routine utilization of locoregional treatment of the primary tumor and ablative treatment for metastatic sites have not been demonstrated to improve
survival outcomes in prospective trials, however, may reduce the risk of local progression and palliate symptoms. Compared with recurrent oligometastatic disease, patients with de novo oligometastatic
cancer have better outcomes and therapeutic decisions should be individualized and discussed in a multidisciplinary context. A large number of case series document long-term disease-free survival (DFS)
for patients with de novo oligometastatic breast cancer who received multimodality therapy leading to “no evidence of disease” (NED). For de novo oligometastatic disease with favorable features (HER2+ or
ER+, induced NED status after receipt of optimal multi-agent systemic regimens, limited sites of metastases, bone-only metastases, and young age/longevity), an aggressive multimodality approach involving
locoregional therapy of the primary tumor, ablative treatment to all sites of metastases, and "adjuvant-like" systemic therapy may offer improved long-term PFS and may be considered. (Also for BINV-21)

BINV-28
General Principles
• The management unresectable, stage IV (M1) or metastatic disease requires a
multifaceted approach, encompassing treatment and a comprehensive survivorship
plan.
• For principles of monitoring metastatic disease, see BINV-R.
• Encourage patients to promptly report any new or worsening symptoms. A
comprehensive evaluation is essential, as these symptoms could indicate disease
progression, treatment effects, or other unrelated medical issues.
• Additional health monitoring, management, and immunizations should be performed
as appropriate to the prognosis under the care of a PCP.
• Patients are encouraged to maintain a therapeutic relationship with a PCP throughout
their lifetime.
Treatment Management and Supportive Care
• Regular follow-up discussions about goals of care and care coordination may be
necessary to improve both survival and quality of life. Engaging patients in these
discussions, including therapy cessation, ensures that care aligns with their values
and preferences.
• Surveillance and follow-up plans should be individualized for all patients, particularly
older patients, considering comorbidities, functional status, and life expectancy.
Personalization of care is key to managing the complexities of older adults or disease
in those with multiple medical comorbidities.
• Patients should receive multidisciplinary and interprofessional care that seeks to
prevent and/or manage morbidities associated with breast cancer or its treatment.
• Patients often experience multiple transitions between different care settings and
health care providers. Coordination between primary care providers and specialists is
essential to ensure continuity of care.
• Addressing all treatment options at each progression, including the option of
supportive care alone or hospice care, is appropriate in patients with stage IV
disease.
• Early involvement of a palliative care team should be considered to provide advance
care planning, goals of care discussions, care transitions, psychosocial support, and
management of cancer and therapy-related symptoms.
• Routine screening, evaluation, and assessment for supportive care interventions and
referrals are necessary. Some of the symptoms are:
Hormone-related symptoms (NCCN Guidelines for Survivorship)
Cognitive dysfunction (NCCN Guidelines for Survivorship)
Peripheral neuropathy
Cancer-related pain (NCCN Guidelines for Adult Cancer Pain)
Cancer-related fatigue (NCCN Guidelines for Cancer-Related Fatigue)
Cancer-related infections (NCCN Guidelines for Prevention and Treatment of
Cancer-Related Infections)
Chemotherapy-induced anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia (NCCN
Guidelines for Hematopoietic Growth Factors)
Immunotherapy-related toxicities (NCCN Guidelines for Management of Immune
Checkpoint Inhibitor-Related Toxicities)
PRINCIPLES OF SURVIVORSHIP FOR THOSE LIVING WITH ADVANCED OR METASTATIC BREAST CANCER
Cardiac monitoring for patients treated with anthracyclines or HER2-
targeted therapy (NCCN Guidelines for Survivorship)
Nausea/vomiting, anorexia, cachexia, diarrhea, dyspnea, malignant
wounds, sleep-wake disturbances (NCCN Guidelines for Antiemesis and
other NCCN Guidelines for Supportive Care)
Lifestyle, Wellness, and Psychosocial Support:
• Patients should be encouraged to maintain a physically active lifestyle and a
healthy diet whenever possible. See NCCN Guidelines for Survivorship.
• Consider appropriate cancer and preventive health screenings per patient
age and sex assigned-at-birth. These decisions should be individualized
according to the patients prognosis.
• Screen for anxiety, fear of progression, depression, and post-traumatic
stress disorder (PTSD) related to cancer and its treatment. See
NCCN Guidelines for Distress Management and NCCN Guidelines for
Survivorship.
• Social determinants of health monitoring and referrals
Provide resources and assistance through financial navigators, social
workers, or other specified personnel to address financial concerns,
employment issues, and family support. Ensuring access to these services
can alleviate significant stress for patients.
◊See NCCN Guidelines for Palliative Care: Social Support/Resource
Management.
◊Financial Advocacy Services Guidelines (Association of Cancer Care
Centers).
◊Financial Toxicity and Cancer Treatment (National Cancer Institute).
See NCCN Guidelines for Distress Management.
• Facilitate connections with consumer groups, support networks, and
organizations that advocate for accessible and equitable care. Engagement
with these groups can empower patients and provide additional support.
• For any patients with sexual health concerns or for fertility and birth control
in premenopausal patients, see BINV-C.
Communication
• Consistent and clear communication while acknowledging uncertainties of
disease trajectory and prognosis is crucial.
• Treatment goals, survivorship issues, and the specific roles of each provider
should be communicated to the patient.
Engagement
• See NCCN Resources for Patients and Caregivers and Guidelines for
Patients.
• Address long-term survivorship issues specific to younger patients, such as
psychosocial development and educational or vocational support. These
patients may face unique challenges that require targeted interventions.
• Living will/end-of-life planning should be discussed.
